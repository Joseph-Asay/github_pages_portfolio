Identifying Basketball Possessions Using Python

Author

Joseph Asay

Introduction

In the NBA, every shot, rebound, foul, and turnover is recorded as a play-by-play event. These records tell us what happened, but analyzing a game one event at a time can make it difficult to see the bigger picture.

A possession is a team’s opportunity to score before control of the ball changes. It might involve one shot, or several if the offense collects its own rebounds. Grouping events into possessions helps analysts examine how teams use those opportunities and eventually measure offensive efficiency.

Packages such as pbpstats can process NBA possessions automatically. Here, we’ll build a simplified version ourselves with pandas, using a Utah Jazz game to learn how event-level data becomes something we can analyze by possession.

Get and Sort the Play-by-Play Data

We’ll use Utah’s 2025–26 regular-season game at Miami (game ID 0022500763). The nba_api package provides a PlayByPlayV3 endpoint where each row describes one event, including its period, clock, team, and event type.

Before identifying possessions, we need the events in the right order. We sort by period and actionNumber rather than by the clock alone: several events, such as a basket and a foul, can share a timestamp.

import pandas as pd
from nba_api.stats.endpoints import playbyplayv3

pbp = playbyplayv3.PlayByPlayV3(
    game_id="0022500763"
).get_data_frames()[0]

pbp = pbp.sort_values(
    ["period", "actionNumber"]
).reset_index(drop=True)

print(pbp[[
    "period", "clock", "teamTricode", "actionType", "description"
]].head(8).to_string(index=False))

As a quick double-check, the game clock should generally count down within each period. Looking at the first few rows confirms that the events follow the expected sequence, even when two plays occur at the same time.

Identify Possession-Ending Events

A missed shot alone does not end a possession: an offensive rebound keeps the ball with the same team. We need events that tell us when the offense’s opportunity actually ends.

Event What happens to the possession?
Turnover Ends
Made field goal Usually ends; an and-one needs special care
Defensive rebound Ends the team that missed the shot’s possession
Offensive rebound Continues
Made final regular free throw Usually ends
Missed shot or missed final free throw Depends on what happens next

Technical fouls, flagrant fouls, and certain team rebounds introduce exceptions. To keep this tutorial focused, we’ll handle the common events and clearly acknowledge what our rules miss.

Distinguish Defensive from Offensive Rebounds

The API’s rebound subType is Unknown in this game. Fortunately, player descriptions include cumulative totals, such as Nurkic REBOUND (Off:2 Def:1). By comparing each player’s defensive total with their previous recorded total, we can flag individual defensive rebounds.

is_rebound = pbp["actionType"].eq("Rebound")

# Extract cumulative offensive and defensive rebound totals
reb_totals = pbp.loc[is_rebound, "description"].str.extract(
    r"Off:(\d+)\s+Def:(\d+)"
).apply(pd.to_numeric)
reb_totals.columns = ["off", "def"]

previous_def = (
    reb_totals.groupby(pbp.loc[is_rebound, "personId"])["def"]
    .shift()
    .fillna(0)
)

pbp["def_rebound"] = False
pbp.loc[is_rebound, "def_rebound"] = reb_totals["def"].gt(previous_def)

print(pbp.loc[is_rebound, [
    "clock", "description", "def_rebound"
]].head(8).to_string(index=False))
      clock                   description  def_rebound
PT11M29.00S    Ware REBOUND (Off:0 Def:1)         True
PT11M19.00S  Nurkic REBOUND (Off:0 Def:1)         True
PT11M00.00S  Nurkic REBOUND (Off:1 Def:1)        False
PT10M58.00S  Nurkic REBOUND (Off:2 Def:1)        False

Notice that a Jazz Rebound without a credited player is marked False. That doesn’t prove it was offensive; our method simply cannot classify team rebounds this way. Some represent possession changes, while others are administrative entries between free throws.

Handle Free Throws and And-Ones

A made final free throw in a regular two- or three-shot sequence usually ends the possession. A missed final attempt does not necessarily end it; the next rebound matters.

There is also an and-one - a made field goal followed by one additional free throw. In this particular game, we checked that all three Free Throw 1 of 1 events were and-ones. We’ll keep each basket and its additional free throw together, rather than treating them as two possessions. This assumption should be rechecked for other games.

is_ft = pbp["actionType"].eq("Free Throw")
made_ft = is_ft & ~pbp["description"].str.startswith("MISS", na=False)

pbp["made_final_ft"] = made_ft & pbp["subType"].isin([
    "Free Throw 2 of 2", "Free Throw 3 of 3"
])
pbp["and_one_ft"] = is_ft & pbp["subType"].eq("Free Throw 1 of 1")

# Match an and-one free throw to a basket by period, clock, and team
has_and_one = pbp.groupby(
    ["period", "clock", "teamTricode"]
)["and_one_ft"].transform("any")
pbp["and_one_basket"] = pbp["actionType"].eq("Made Shot") & has_and_one

Assign Possession IDs

Now we can combine our indicators with | (logical OR). We exclude the made basket in an and-one and instead end the possession at its made free throw, or at a later defensive rebound if that free throw misses.

pbp["possession_end"] = (
    pbp["actionType"].eq("Turnover")
    | (pbp["actionType"].eq("Made Shot") & ~pbp["and_one_basket"])
    | pbp["def_rebound"]
    | pbp["made_final_ft"]
    | (pbp["and_one_ft"] & made_ft)
)

# Begin the next ID after an ending event, or at a new period
previous_period = pbp["period"].shift()
new_possession = (
    pbp["period"].ne(previous_period)
    | (
        pbp["possession_end"].shift(fill_value=False)
        & pbp["period"].eq(previous_period)
    )
)
pbp["possession_id"] = new_possession.cumsum()

The key trick is .shift() followed by .cumsum(). Shifting starts the next ID after the ending event; the cumulative sum numbers each resulting group. In this convention, a defensive rebound remains the final event of the opponent’s offensive possession. Also, teamTricode identifies the team involved in an event, not necessarily the team that was on offense for the entire possession.

Check One Possession

Let’s inspect possession 3 from the first quarter to verify the result.

print(pbp.loc[
    pbp["possession_id"].eq(3),
    ["clock", "actionType", "description", "possession_id"]
].to_string(index=False))
      clock  actionType                             description  possession_id
PT11M03.00S Missed Shot MISS Markkanen 25' 3PT Pullup Jump Shot              3
PT11M00.00S     Rebound            Nurkic REBOUND (Off:1 Def:1)              3
PT10M58.00S Missed Shot            MISS Nurkic 3' Putback Layup              3
PT10M58.00S     Rebound            Nurkic REBOUND (Off:2 Def:1)              3
PT10M58.00S        Foul       Ware S.FOUL (P1.T1) (J.Schroeder)              3

All eight events receive possession ID 3. Utah misses two shots, Nurkić grabs two offensive rebounds, and the Jazz then attempt two free throws. Miami’s defensive rebound after Nurkić misses the second free throw finally ends the possession. That’s the behavior we wanted to capture.

Conclusion

We started with individual play-by-play records, identified common possession-ending events, and used pandas to label groups of plays with possession IDs. Those IDs make it possible to build possession-level summaries with groupby() and investigate questions such as how often teams get second chances.

This is a teaching model, not an official possession counter. Team rebounds, unusual foul situations, and other edge cases can make some boundaries inaccurate. For production analysis, we’d need additional rules and validation.

Try it yourself: Swap in another NBA game ID, inspect a few consecutive possessions, and see where your rules succeed or need improvement. You can compare your approach with pbpstats as a next step.
